Course Reflection

I’d imagine that some individuals who have previously (or are currently) working in a library may be familiar with at least a few of the documents that we have learned to create and analyze this semester. However, I began this semester with little to no knowledge of classification systems, controlled vocabularies, content schemas, and data formats for bibliographic description. Aware that I had a lot to learn, I turned in all of the weekly assignments on time so that I could participate in the peer review process. Thus, LIBR 509 has had a very significant impact on my development.

Prior to this course, I was familiar with Dewey and LCC in that I could use the call numbers to find a book in the library. However, I would not have been able to tell you the difference between hierarchical and faceted systems. Through the classification system-create assignment, I was able to test my understanding of principles we learned through lectures: mutual exclusivity, homogeneity, and joint exhaustivity. This proved challenging for my collection, as I struggled to create a singular facet that described the type of instruction (assembly, operating, and installation) within each manual. After receiving peer feedback, I decided to create three facets for this purpose instead of one. I also provided peer feedback for three of my classmates, pointing out where there were mutual exclusivity or maintenance issues. The assignment to analyze a classification system strengthened my understanding in a new way. Seeing a classification system in action—outside of the library context—gave me insight into the ethical issues that inevitably follow when we organize information and works of art.

As mentioned in the learning outcomes section of this assignment, I absolutely loved creating a thesaurus for romance books. Without actually defining the relationships between terms myself, I’m not sure I would have been able to move onto the next step of analyzing a controlled vocabulary. I feel very confident in my knowledge of thesauri, especially in terms of the reciprocity requirement. In my peer reviews for classmates, I pointed out a number of reciprocity issues in their documents, even attaching documents to help them visualize the improvements. If I were to focus on any aspect of resource description after this semester, I would be most interested in working with thesauri.

In terms of the content schema, I was very ambitious with the create assignment. I created a system that could (hopefully) be applied to any location within the Getty Thesaurus of Geographic Names. I struggled to be inspired at first, purely using the information within the thesaurus to guide the attributes. Part of what challenged me was finding relevant controlled vocabularies that could fill the value space. Because there’s a lot of variation between locations, I felt that it was very important to control the format. After the round of peer reviews, I became even more specific with format, and I added climate-related attributes. As for the analysis assignment, I spent a lot of time browsing content standards before deciding on a subject. Through this process, I became more familiar with the extreme breadth of most standards; the sheer detail was quite overwhelming. However, I think that the hours I spent just reading through content standards informed my analysis of LOM-SG.

BIBFRAME took a while for me to wrap my mind around. It’s difficult to visualize functionality that is not currently available, so when the literature was describing linked data, I initially had a hard time understanding what this would mean for the web. Although I mention in my assignment that BIBFRAME is advantageous in terms of reaching new library patrons, I do wonder whether linked data would really provide a significant boost in usage. People who find library records via the web would still need to get a library card to access the resource, although some libraries have made it easy to apply online and instantly access e-books. Even if linked data wouldn't significantly boost usage, it would certainly be more convenient to use one search engine to find any relevant information. I will definitely be monitoring the development of BIBFRAME from now on, and I am confident that I would be able to use both BIBFRAME and MARC if I had access to documents for reference (i.e. I have not memorized all of the numerical meanings in MARC, but similar to coding, access to the web for questions makes a big difference.)

Lastly, bringing all of these systems together in the integration assignments was fun and rewarding. They were a reminder of all that we had accomplished throughout the semester. I especially enjoyed creating the Buffy integrated system, and I think it’s a great demonstration of my competence. I’m grateful to this course for providing enjoyable means of learning, and I feel confident in the skills that I have gained. I will be taking UBC’s cataloging class next semester, where I suspect we will build on the knowledge from LIBR 509. Thank you for a great course!
